3a. Who is responsible for what (GDPR)

When a session is recorded, the server administrator who runs it is the data controller: they decide to record, why, and what to do with the result. Lestapenna acts as a processor on their instructions, and the AI provider is a sub-processor under their key. This is not a way of passing the buck — it is the correct allocation, and it comes with obligations on our side:

  • Purpose limitation. Audio and transcripts are processed only to produce the transcript, the recap and the campaign archive of that table. They are never used to train models, never sold, never shared for anyone's marketing.
  • Retention. Raw audio is deleted automatically after 30 days, or sooner once the session archive exists. Transcripts and campaign data are kept while the server keeps using the Bot, so its archive keeps working.
  • Sub-processors are listed in full in the Privacy Policy. Where you self-host, most of them disappear.
  • Security. Provider keys are encrypted at rest with per-tenant derived keys; no endpoint ever returns their value.
  • Deletion. Anyone at the table can erase their own data with $forgetme, and get a copy of it with $mydata, without asking us or anyone else. An administrator can erase the whole server with $eraseserver. Removing the Bot from a server erases that server's data automatically — recordings, transcripts, recaps and every stored file.

What remains yours to do: tell the people at your table that the session is being recorded, and let anyone who does not want to take part step out. The Bot makes this as visible as it can — it marks its own name while recording, refuses to record at all if it cannot display that marker, and posts a notice in the channel naming the commands to get a copy of your data or erase it — but it cannot obtain consent on your behalf.

4a. Pictures you upload

You can attach pictures to a campaign: a portrait on a character's sheet, a reference image that tells the AI what a place or a piece of armour looks like. Three things follow from that, and the first is the one that matters.

  • Upload only what you have the right to upload. Your own work, something you hold a licence for, or something whose licence permits this use. Art found through a search engine is almost never any of those. By uploading you state that you have that right, and that the picture does not infringe anyone else's.
  • It stays yours. We claim no ownership over it, exactly as with the rest of your campaign material. What you give us is a narrow permission — to store it, convert it to WebP, show it back to the members of your own table, and, only for the pictures you explicitly tick as references, send it to the image model your table has configured. Nothing else: it is never published, never shared with anyone outside your table, never used to train anything, and never used to promote this project. The permission ends when the picture does — delete it and it is gone from our storage too.
  • What must not go up: anything unlawful; material that infringes somebody's copyright, trademark or image rights; photographs of people who have not agreed to be there.

Nobody looks at your pictures before they are stored. There is no prior screening and no automated scanning — uploads are not inspected, and we have no general obligation to monitor them. That is the honest description of how this works, and it is the reason section 4b exists: we cannot know that something should not be there until somebody tells us, and when somebody does, we act.

You can delete any picture yourself from the sheet it is attached to. Everything you uploaded also goes with $forgetme, with the campaign, and with the server when the Bot is removed — from the database and from the file storage alike.

If a third party brings a claim against us because of material you uploaded, you are responsible for it and will hold us harmless, to the extent the applicable law allows — and where your local consumer law says otherwise, that law wins.
